## The Week in One Paragraph

### Seven Days, One Direction

The weekly AI news roundup for November 23 covers November 16 to 22, and every story points the same way. Capability went up, the price per task fell through the floor, and the cost of owning the machine that produces it went up faster.

### The Market Finally Did the Math

Anthropic held the biggest IPO in history on Thursday. By Friday the Nasdaq had erased all of its 2026 gains, and roughly $4 trillion in market value was gone for the week. The listing that was supposed to validate the AI trade became the event that drained it.

### The Bill Had Three Line Items

The same week brought a 32-million-tonne carbon estimate for Anthropic's 2028 power, a California subpoena over Nvidia chips reaching China, and a critical Java bug that sat in public for five months. None of these are model stories. All of them set the real cost of the boom.

## Anthropic's IPO Was the Liquidity Event, Not the Rescue

### The Numbers From the First Two Days

Anthropic priced its shares at $83 on Wednesday and began trading Thursday. The stock jumped 17% on day one after briefly rising almost 40%. On Friday it fell 9% to $88.30, barely above the offer price.

That is a strange outcome for the largest IPO on record. The company's seven co-founders were reported to be worth $180 billion. The market valued the business near $1.3 trillion.

### The Index Did the Opposite of What IPOs Are Supposed to Do

A huge listing usually pulls money into its sector. This one pulled money out. The Nasdaq Composite closed Friday down 5% from its value on January 1, erasing every gain made in 2026.

The damage was global and broad. Nvidia fell 14%, SanDisk 14%, Micron 12% and TSMC about 6%. Japan's Nikkei lost 5%, South Korea's Kospi 5.5%, and SoftBank 14.5% in Tokyo. The FTSE 100 had its worst week since the start of the Iran war.

### Why the "Stabilizing Bid" Story Matters

Our [November 22 briefing](https://blogs.thetrillioniar.me/blog/2026-11-22-ai-news-carbon-bill-zero-day) covered the detail that explains the speed of the drop. The bank running the IPO closed the position meant to stabilise the stock. Once that bid was gone, the only buyer left was the public.

IG's Chris Beauchamp gave the mechanism a name: liquidity drain. Investors sold other tech names to fund their Anthropic allocations. That is why an AI company's best day became the rest of the sector's worst week.

### The Signal for Every AI Company Still Private

Anthropic had the strongest possible story: frontier models, government customers, a public benefit charter. It still could not hold its first-day pop for 48 hours. Every private AI lab now has a harder conversation with its next investor.

## Nvidia's $104 Billion Quarter Came With a Subpoena

### The Disclosure That Disappeared

Nvidia reported quarterly revenue of $104 billion, according to the Financial Times. In the same filing it stopped breaking out sales to Singapore. Singapore is not a large end market for AI chips. It is a transshipment point.

Removing that line removes the ability to see how much product leaves through the route most associated with diversion to China. The timing was not subtle.

### California Issued the Subpoena on Thursday

Attorney General Rob Bonta served Nvidia with an investigative subpoena on November 19. His office is examining whether advanced chips reached China despite US export controls.

Bonta also asked whether Nvidia misled investors about that risk. "Companies cannot mislead their investors," he said. The inquiry followed the November 12 resignation of CEO Jensen Huang.

### The Megaspeed Thread

Huang left after disclosures about multiple affairs, including one with Alice Huang, a suspected Chinese spy. She is associated with Singapore-based Megaspeed, which bought nearly $5 billion of Nvidia chips and hardware.

Megaspeed denies wrongdoing and says it is a lawful business. Huang is due to testify before several congressional committees on December 1. Nvidia's chair has said the company will not reinstate him.

### What This Does to the "AI Trade"

Nvidia's shares are down about a fifth since the affair became public, wiping almost $1 trillion in market value. On Thursday they hit a one-year low. AMD fell almost 8% the same day after a short-seller report on its China links.

The chip layer is no longer a clean way to own AI. It is now a national-security story with a balance sheet attached.

## Every Model Got Cheaper, and That Is the Whole Strategy

### Six Launches in Seven Days

Alibaba shipped Qwen4-Max on November 16. OpenAI shipped GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol on November 17. Microsoft shipped seven models the same day. Google shipped Gemini 4 Neon on November 18. Thinking Machines shipped Inkling 2 Mini on November 20.

No single launch was the story. The pattern was. Every lab led with cost per task, not benchmark rank.

### The New Price Floor

GPT-6.2 Astra costs $8 per million input tokens, $0.40 for cached input, and $40 for output. That is 20% below GPT-6 Astra, with a 60% cache discount. GPT-6.2 Sol holds at $2, $0.10 and $10.

Gemini 4 Neon undercuts that at $3, $0.15 and $15, with a 1 million-token window. Google says it costs 32% less per task than Gemini 4 Carbon.

Microsoft went further. It claims MAI Cyber 2 Flash matches GPT-6 Astra's quality at under 2% of the cost per task on CyberGym-E2E. MAI Code 2 reportedly reaches 96% of Claude Opus 5.5's performance at 5% of the price.

### The Small Model Is Now the Interesting One

Inkling 2 Mini is the clearest technical result of the week. It has 26.4 billion total parameters, 3.4 billion active, and a memory footprint of about 20 gigabytes. It runs offline on a MacBook Pro.

The trick is architectural. The model loops its two middle blocks three times per token, so it reasons longer without writing the reasoning down. It scores 48.2% on DeepSWE v1.1, ahead of every open-weights model under 100 billion parameters.

That matters for a specific reason. A frontier-class coding agent that runs on a laptop breaks the assumption that capability must be rented from a data centre.

### The Supply Side Is Collapsing Too

Architect Labs announced Frostbite on November 18, a general-purpose CPU designed end to end by AI. It reached 4.59 times the performance per watt of a modern superscalar core across 25 workloads, including 15 held out until the final test.

Microsoft put its Maia 200 accelerator into production at 1.7 times more tokens per watt. If both claims hold, the cost of serving a token falls from three directions at once: better models, cheaper weights, and more efficient silicon.

### Why Cheap Inference Is Not Good News for Capex

Cheaper tokens grow usage, but they also shrink the revenue needed to pay for the buildout. The industry is spending on gas turbines, fabs and data centres at 2024 prices while selling inference at 2026 prices.

That gap is the real AI bubble question. This week the market started pricing it.

## The Carbon Bill Arrived Three Days Before the IPO

### The 87% Number

The Better Data Center Project published a site-by-site analysis of Anthropic's compute on November 16. It estimates that about 87% of the company's power will come from gas and 11% from coal in 2028.

Only 2% is left for everything else. That is not a rounding error in a sustainability report. It is the physical composition of the power behind Claude.

### 32 Million Tonnes Is One Company

The report puts power emissions at 17 to 38 million tonnes of CO2 equivalent in 2028, with a mid-case of 32 million. California's entire power sector emitted 31.1 million tonnes in 2024.

Meta, Microsoft and Google reported combined location-based Scope 2 emissions of about 32.9 million tonnes in 2025. One company's projected 2028 footprint lands in the same range as three hyperscalers combined.

### The Contract Numbers Behind It

Anthropic had contracted 15 gigawatts of compute as of October 2026. Between 5.9 and 9.6 gigawatts is tied to specific sites. The upper figure could power about seven million homes, or 18% of California's peak demand.

In the US, the company has bought commitments for 1.7 gigawatts of heavy-duty gas generators for $3.4 billion. Its disclosed renewable contracts are in Norway and Canada.

### The Health Cost Is Local

About 80% of the on-site capacity uses simple-cycle turbines and engines, which pollute more per unit of electricity than combined-cycle plants. The report estimates $2 to $3 billion in annual health damages from NOx and fine particulates.

The average carbon intensity of Anthropic's power is estimated at 1,033 to 1,076 pounds of CO2 per megawatt-hour. The US grid average is 767.

### Why the Timing Is the Story

The report landed on Monday. The IPO priced on Wednesday. Investors were asked to value a company whose largest disclosed physical input had just been costed by an outside group, and the prospectus did not contain that number.

## AI Proved the Riemann Hypothesis. Nobody Can Read the Proof.

### What Was Released on November 20

Levent Alpöge released a Lean library of 17 million lines that proves the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis. The Riemann Hypothesis is the most famous special case.

Terence Tao described it as generated by an internal AI model, with a proof strategy that appears to use modern work on the Langlands program and arithmetic geometry.

### The Community Certified It in About a Day

The Lean and Mathlib communities issued a joint statement on November 21. They confirmed four things: the statement is correct, the library compiles, the theorem matches Mathlib's statement, and independent kernels accept the proof.

Two checkers, `con-leche` and `con-ron`, come with formal proofs that they cannot accept a false proof. That closes the obvious objection that Lean's own logic might be the weak link.

### Verification Is Not Understanding

This is the part most coverage skipped. The community verified that the proof is valid. Nobody has yet explained, in human terms, why the argument works.

Tao was explicit about the ordering. The Euler and Navier-Stokes results have human-readable papers and clear strategies. The GRH result is a Lean-only artifact whose digestion "will take some time."

Mathematics now has a category of result that is certainly true and not yet understood. That is new.

### The Physics Result Shows the Other Path

Anthropic published a different kind of AI research on November 17. Claude solved two open problems in black hole physics, including a calculation physicists set up in 2003 and never finished.

The agents ran for about six weeks on 22 candidate problems. One problem had already failed in 61 parallel attempts before a different agent found the pattern. The results were formalised in Lean in September and checked by a physicist.

### Why the Difference Matters

In the physics case, a human can follow the paper. In the GRH case, the proof is a compiled object larger than most software projects. The first is AI as a research assistant. The second is AI as an oracle that happens to be checkable.

Both are useful. Only one is science in the sense most mathematicians mean.

## Java4Shell Sat in Public for Five Months

### The Bug Is in the Base64 Decoder

Calif disclosed Java4Shell, CVE-2026-114938, on November 17. It is a memory-corruption flaw in OpenJDK's x86-64 Base64 intrinsic, rated 9.2 under CVSS 4.0.

The trigger is 64 letter A characters followed by a single equals sign. A miscalculated write mask sends 62 bytes past the end of a 50-byte buffer. That is enough for a heap leak and, with grooming, remote code execution.

### The List of Affected Products Is the Economy

Calif demonstrated pre-authentication RCE in Keycloak, Atlassian Jira, Confluence, JFrog Artifactory, Jenkins and PingFederate. It demonstrated post-authentication RCE in Kafka, Solr, Elasticsearch, Gerrit and Spinnaker.

Java runs in banking, aviation, telecom and national security systems. The flaw affects OpenJDK 17.0.6 and later on x86-64 chips with AVX-512 VBMI support. ARM is not affected.

### It Took AI Under an Hour to Find

Calif's Quang Luong asked Codex to audit the JVM on October 24. The model found the bug within an hour. The first working exploit took two more hours, for a total token cost under $200.

The bug was not hidden by the AI. It was hidden by a broken process.

### The Part That Should Worry You

A user named `sundarms` hit the bug in the wild on June 9 and opened a public report. A crash reproducer followed in September. A contributor named `albertnetymk` found the root cause and opened a fix.

That pull request was closed without being merged. The bug shipped for five more months. Oracle patched it on November 17, after Calif showed that it was exploitable.

### The Lesson Is About Incentives, Not Talent

The bug had a public report, a reproducer, a diagnosis and a patch. What it lacked was a reason for anyone to prioritise it. A working exploit supplied that reason in three hours.

Expect more of this. AI has collapsed the cost of finding vulnerabilities. It has not collapsed the cost of caring about them.

## The Web Is Paying for Its Own Replacement

### The 40% Number Is From a Controlled Experiment

Researchers Saharsh Agarwal and Ananya Sen hid AI Overviews from a random group of US desktop Chrome users. They then compared two weeks of real searches in early 2026.

On searches that would show an Overview, the group that saw them made 0.37 outbound clicks. The group that did not made 0.62. That is a 39.8% reduction. Searches ending with no outbound click rose from 54% to 73%.

### The Scale of the Substitution

DataForSEO's panel found AI Overviews on 61.43% of tracked keywords in August 2026. In September 2024 the figure was 5.93%.

Desktop click-through rate for the first organic result fell from 17.83% in September 2025 to 7.43% in August 2026. A top ranking now earns less than half the clicks it did a year earlier.

### AI Assistants Are Not Replacing the Traffic

Ahrefs measured AI assistants across 74,752 websites in March 2026. They sent 0.28% of total traffic, about 3.5 million visitors against 345 million from Google.

So the clicks are disappearing faster than they are being replaced. The web is funding the system that reads it.

### The Shortcut Nobody Can Prove

Sites have spent the year adding `llms.txt` files and AI-specific markup. SE Ranking analysed nearly 300,000 domains and found no correlation with citations. SISTRIX reached the same conclusion.

Google says no special files or markup are required. The data agrees. What correlates is the boring stuff: original data, quotations, sources, and a visible last-updated date.

### Why This Belongs in an AI News Roundup

Every model launch this week was also a publishing decision. A cheaper model means more AI answers, which means fewer clicks, which means less money for the reporting those answers are built on.

The loop is not hypothetical. It is measurable, and this week it got cheaper to run.

## Windows Counts Which Coding Agent You Use

### The Component and the Feature ID

A reverse-engineering write-up published November 16 found coding-agent names inside `MissionControlAggregator.dll`. The feature is called `CLICodingAgentUsage`, ID 62580080.

The DLL checks for `codex.exe`, `claude.exe`, `copilot.exe` and `agy.exe` in command-line slots. It checks `cursor.exe` and `antigravity.exe` in desktop slots. It also checks for Python, Node, GCC, Clang, Java and Visual Studio.

### It Is On by Default

The researcher tested a clean Windows 11 ARM64 install with the region set to the United States. The diagnostic data toggle was already set to "Include Optional," and `AllowTelemetry` was 3.

With optional diagnostics on, the report is queued for upload to Microsoft. It records a bitmap of which tools were seen, not how long they ran or what they did.

### The Detail That Makes It Work

The component does not scan processes. It subscribes to existing Windows events, including `KernelProcess.AppStateChangeSummary` and WSL execution events.

Copying `cmd.exe` to a file named `codex.exe` was enough to set the Codex bit. The name is the signal.

### Why a Coding-Agent Census Is Commercially Interesting

Microsoft sells Copilot. Knowing which agent a developer actually runs is competitive intelligence that no survey can match. It is also a privacy question that most users never agreed to answer.

The fix is one toggle under Settings, Privacy and security, Diagnostics and feedback. Most people will never find it.

## What Was Noise This Week

### Big Stories That Were Not AI Stories

A pancreatic cancer pill was hailed as the biggest breakthrough in decades. Toyota, Nissan and Honda agreed to standardise parts. Carnegie Mellon put its computer science courses on YouTube for free.

All three were genuinely important. None of them changed what AI can do, what it costs, or who controls it. A weekly roundup earns its name by dropping them.

### The AI Story That Was Mostly Vibes

"All AI-generated code should be open source" drew 1,288 points and a long argument. It is a reasonable position, but it is a position. It did not change a licence, a benchmark or a product this week.

### The One We Are Still Watching

Microsoft's claim that MAI Cyber 2 Flash matches GPT-6 Astra at under 2% of the cost per task is the most consequential number of the week. It is also a vendor claim on a vendor benchmark. Independent runs will decide whether it holds.

## FAQ

### What happened in AI news this week?

Anthropic held the largest IPO in history and the Nasdaq erased its 2026 gains. Nvidia reported $104 billion in revenue and received a California subpoena over China exports. Six labs shipped cheaper frontier models, and Java4Shell was patched after five months of public exposure.

### Why did Anthropic's IPO hurt the market?

It drained liquidity. Investors sold other tech stocks to buy the new listing, and the bank's stabilising bid closed after the first day. The Nasdaq fell 5% for the year and about $4 trillion in value disappeared across the week.

### Is AI getting cheaper or more expensive?

Both. The price per token and per task fell sharply this week. The cost of the physical buildout rose, with gas turbines, data centres and chip capacity contracted years in advance. Cheap inference and expensive infrastructure are now the same business.

### Was the Riemann Hypothesis really proved by AI?

The Lean and Mathlib communities certified a 17-million-line Lean proof released by Levent Alpöge, which Terence Tao says was generated with an internal AI model. The proof is verified. The human explanation of why it works does not exist yet.

### What is Java4Shell and should I patch?

Java4Shell is CVE-2026-114938, a CVSS 9.2 memory-corruption flaw in OpenJDK's Base64 decoder on x86-64 chips with AVX-512 VBMI. It allows remote code execution in Jenkins, Keycloak, Jira and other Java applications. Patch OpenJDK 17.0.6 and later immediately.

## The Bottom Line

### What Lasted

Three things survived the week's noise. Price per task is now the number every lab competes on. Verification without comprehension is a real category in mathematics. And the security patch rate, not the bug discovery rate, is the bottleneck.

### What to Watch Next

Watch Nvidia's December 1 congressional testimony and the California investigation. Watch whether Anthropic publishes a US renewable contract that contradicts the 87% gas estimate. Watch independent benchmarks for Microsoft's 2%-of-cost claim.

Watch the first GRH explainer that a human can read. That will be the real proof that the result changed mathematics rather than just formalising it.

### The Pattern This Week

Every headline had a bill attached. The IPO bill was liquidity. The Nvidia bill was export control. The model bill was capex. The carbon bill was 32 million tonnes. The security bill was five months of an unmerged pull request.

AI is very good at shipping capability and very slow at publishing what it consumes. This was the week the second number arrived.
